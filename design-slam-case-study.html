Design Education · Experiential Learning at EY

Teaching consultants to think like designers, one evening at a time

EY hired brilliant consultants straight out of college, but very few had ever designed anything. With my colleagues Racine Davis and Kelcie Wilderman, I co-founded the Design Slam: a hands-on, after-hours design event built on Stanford d.school design thinking, open to anyone at EY who was curious.

Context

EY's new hires were highly educated and quick to learn. What most of them lacked was any experience of the design process.
Consultants joined EY directly from top universities and went straight onto client work, where they were increasingly expected to take part in design conversations. Few had sketched an interface, framed a user problem or had their work critiqued by a designer.
There are plenty of ways to learn about design: books, courses, lectures and case studies. Racine, Kelcie and I believed design is best learned by doing it. So instead of another training deck, we built an event where people would design something, show it and talk about it, all in one evening.

The challenge

We needed a format that would teach real design skills to busy consultants, outside working hours, across two cities.
Goal 01

Hands-on

Participants should leave having designed something, not just having heard about design.

Goal 02

Open to everyone

No prior experience required. Anyone at EY with an interest in design was welcome.

Goal 03

One team, two offices

Charlotte and Manhattan had to feel like a single event, sharing the same prompt, timer and critique.

How might we give people with no design background a real taste of the design process, in a single evening?

Grounded in design thinking

We based the curriculum on the Stanford d.school's five-stage design thinking model, giving participants a process they could reuse on client work the next morning.
Stage 01

Empathize

Understand who you're designing for, their motivations and their problems.

Stage 02

Define

Frame the user's need as a clear problem worth solving.

Stage 03

Ideate

Generate many possible solutions quickly, before settling on one.

Stage 04

Prototype

Make ideas tangible through sketching and wireframing.

Stage 05

Test

Put the concept in front of others and learn from their reactions.

The program focused on three things participants would learn: rapid iteration, how to articulate a design concept, and a base knowledge of concept design.

Anatomy of a slam

Every session followed the same five-step rhythm. Keeping the structure fixed let us change the prompt each month without re-teaching the format.
  1. 01

    Provide background

    We introduced the problem space and the pain points we wanted people to consider, then opened the floor for questions about audience and context.

  2. 02

    Distribute the prompt

    A single "How might we…" question, given to both offices at the same moment.

  3. 03

    Design

    One hour to sketch and wireframe a concept. The time limit forced people to form ideas quickly and commit to them.

  4. 04

    Share the concept

    Each participant presented their work to the group, in the room and over video.

  5. 05

    Receive feedback

    Senior designers critiqued each concept in real time, and the group discussed what worked.

Primer

Sketching for non-designers

Most participants hadn't drawn since school, so each session opened with a short sketching primer. We explained why designers sketch (to record ideas quickly, make mistakes sooner, communicate better and produce lots of ideas) and broke it down to three building blocks: line, shape and copy.

Session one: notifications

The first slam, held on November 11, 2016, took on a problem everyone with a phone has felt.
We opened with the background: users are bombarded with notifications that distract them from their day, and annoying notifications are the top reason people uninstall mobile apps. Yet well-designed notifications are one of the most powerful ways for a business to reach the right person at the right time. Then we handed out the prompt.
Critique

Teaching people how to talk about design

The critique was where most of the learning happened. Senior designers served as guest judges, and we gave everyone the same five questions to frame their presentation and their feedback. The questions moved the conversation away from "do I like it?" and toward the user and the problem.

Who is this for?
What need does this fill?
What assumptions did you make about platform and context?
What problems does this solve?
How will this impact your users?
Closing

Participants chose what came next

Each session ended with a vote on the next month's prompt. After session one, the group chose between registration and onboarding, account information, and list management. Letting participants pick the problem kept them invested and brought them back the following month.

What a concept looks like

We set expectations with sample concepts: low-fidelity wireframes that show the thinking, not polish.

From pitch to program

We pitched the idea as an internal learning initiative, then shaped it as we ran it.
The original pitch proposed a weekly event over three months. In practice, the Design Slam ran as six monthly sessions, each about three hours with one hour of focused design time. Every session ran in person in our Charlotte and Manhattan offices at the same time, linked by teleconference so both groups worked on the same prompt and heard the same critique.

Reflection

The Design Slam showed that you don't need a design degree to start thinking like a designer. You need a prompt, a deadline and honest feedback.

Design is a verb

People learned more from one hour of sketching than from any amount of explanation. Doing came first, and theory made sense afterward.

Constraints build confidence

The one-hour limit removed the pressure to be perfect. Nobody expected polish, so everybody shipped something.

Critique is the curriculum

Giving everyone the same five questions taught them a shared vocabulary for talking about design that they took back to client work.

Let the room steer

Voting on the next prompt gave participants ownership and kept the program relevant to the problems they cared about.
