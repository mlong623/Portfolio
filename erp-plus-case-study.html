Making enterprise procurement feel consumer-friendly

Context

Enterprise ERP systems are built for procurement specialists — not the nurses, front desk coordinators, and clinic staff who occasionally need to order supplies.
Those occasional users were expected to navigate an interface designed around procurement processes, ERP terminology, and decades of accumulated functionality. The result was predictable: simple purchasing tasks became unnecessarily difficult.
Rather than redesigning the ERP itself, our opportunity was to rethink the experience entirely.

The challenge

We needed to improve the purchasing experience for Acute Care organizations without replacing the Core ERP platform that powered inventory, purchasing, approvals, and business rules.
Constraint 01

The Core ERP remained the system of record. No business logic could change.

Constraint 02

Development resources were limited. Building a replacement ERP wasn't feasible.

Constraint 03

The solution had to support existing ERP integrations while dramatically improving usability.

How might we give occasional purchasers a consumer-grade ordering experience without changing the ERP that powers it?

My role

As Principal Product Designer, I led UX strategy from discovery through implementation — and beyond designing interfaces, I was responsible for helping the team reframe the problem itself.
  • Leading discovery
  • Facilitating stakeholder interviews
  • Synthesizing research
  • Defining UX principles
  • Creating interaction models
  • Designing high-fidelity experiences
  • Presenting design rationale
  • Supporting sprint implementation

Research under real constraints

My preferred approach is always direct user research. Budget and timeline constraints prevented formal interviews with Acute Care customers during discovery.
Rather than guessing, I partnered with the company's field consultants — who collectively represented decades of implementation experience across Acute Care organizations and had observed hundreds of customer deployments and recurring support issues.
Instead of asking what features users wanted, we focused on understanding their daily workflow, the questions they asked most often, common mistakes, existing technology limitations, and where ERP complexity created unnecessary work.

The turning point

Initially, our conversations centered on simplifying ERP screens. After several interviews, a different pattern emerged.

Users weren't asking for a better ERP. They wanted to avoid interacting with the ERP altogether.

That insight fundamentally changed the direction of the project. Instead of redesigning ERP workflows, we designed a completely different experience powered by the existing ERP behind the scenes. Our strategy became: treat the ERP as infrastructure — not as the user experience. Everything that followed was guided by that principle.

Research insights

Six recurring patterns that reshaped what the product needed to be.
01
Insight

"Where is my stuff?"

Tracking order status was the single most important task. Users rarely asked about procurement workflows — they wanted confidence that supplies were arriving when expected.

Design response

Status became the primary organizing principle of the application — the current state of every order, visible immediately.

02
Insight

"Is this the right product?"

Ordering often began with an empty package or a handwritten note. Users struggled to identify products using ERP catalog numbers.

Design response

The interface prioritized recognition over recall — plain-language descriptions, intuitive search, and product context instead of internal ERP identifiers.

03
Insight

ERP assumptions didn't match reality

Traditional ERP systems assume users manage hundreds of requisitions. Acute Care users typically managed fewer than ten.

Design response

Spreadsheet-style interfaces were replaced with lightweight task lists focused on active work, not historical data.

04
Insight

Ordering wasn't their job

Ordering supplies happened between checking patients in, scheduling appointments, and supporting clinicians. Every extra click interrupted higher-value work.

Design response

Every workflow was evaluated against one question: can someone complete this task in under a minute?

05
Insight

Less information, better decisions

Users consistently ignored large amounts of ERP data because it wasn't relevant to their immediate task.

Design response

Rather than exposing every available field, we progressively disclosed information only when users needed it.

06
Insight

The ERP couldn't change

Users didn't need most ERP functionality — but every workflow still depended on the ERP's purchasing rules and integrations.

Design response

We abstracted ERP complexity behind APIs while preserving every underlying business rule.

Design principles

Every decision throughout the project was evaluated against four principles.
01

Show only what users need

Every field had to justify its existence.

02

Prioritize recognition over recall

People shouldn't memorize ERP codes to complete simple tasks.

03

Surface status before actions

Users first wanted to know what was happening. Only then did they want to take action.

04

Let the ERP manage complexity

Users should benefit from enterprise software without needing to understand it.

Solving the right problems

Four pieces of the experience did most of the work.

Simplified ordering

Redesigned around familiar shopping behaviors rather than ERP transactions — users searched naturally, confirmed products visually, and completed purchases with minimal interaction.

Order status dashboard

Instead of searching across multiple ERP screens, users immediately understood what had been ordered, its current status, and any outstanding actions.

ERP translation layer

One of the least visible, most impactful efforts: translating ERP language into terminology familiar to Acute Care users, without breaking Core ERP compatibility.

Progressive disclosure

Rather than removing ERP functionality, we concealed unnecessary complexity until users needed it — preserving capability while cutting cognitive load.

Collaboration & design leadership

One recurring challenge involved existing ERP conventions — Engineering and SMEs understandably preferred interfaces that closely resembled the Core ERP because they were familiar and technically predictable.
I facilitated design reviews grounded in research rather than personal preference. When discussions centered on exposing additional ERP fields or preserving legacy terminology, I used research findings to show how those decisions increased cognitive load without improving task success.
This shifted conversations from "How does the ERP work?" to "What is the user trying to accomplish?" — a shared perspective that significantly improved collaboration throughout implementation.

Design system evolution

Midway through development, the company introduced new branding, followed by a broader enterprise design system.
Instead of simply updating colors and typography, I used the transition as an opportunity to improve interaction consistency, refactor components, and resolve usability issues identified during earlier design reviews. Several patterns introduced through ERP Plus later informed the broader design system.

Business impact

ERP Plus demonstrated that simplifying enterprise software can improve both efficiency and satisfaction without sacrificing capability.

The reduction in task time wasn't the result of one feature — it came from a series of intentional design decisions: simplified navigation, consumer-inspired ordering, reduced cognitive load, better terminology, status-first workflows, and progressive disclosure.
Perhaps the strongest validation came from users outside Acute Care, who requested the same simplified experience for their own workflows. What began as a targeted solution became the catalyst for a broader ERP modernization effort.

Reflection

This project fundamentally changed how I think about enterprise software.
When we began, I believed success meant making ERP systems easier to learn. Research challenged that assumption — users didn't want a better ERP, they wanted to accomplish their work without thinking about the ERP at all.

How can we remove the need for users to understand enterprise software in the first place?

Great enterprise UX exposes purpose, not power

The most successful design decisions reduced what users needed to think about rather than what the system could do.

Constraints can improve design

Working within an immutable ERP forced innovation at the experience layer — a more elegant, maintainable solution than a wholesale redesign.

Research should change strategy, not just interfaces

The project's biggest success wasn't a redesigned screen — it was reframing the product itself, treating the ERP as infrastructure instead of the interface.

Reframing beats simplifying

The best answer to "make it easier to use" was sometimes "make it disappear."
