01The starting point

When I joined Premier in 2009, there was no dedicated UX capability. Across the portfolio, business analysts and developers made most interface decisions. Products shared corporate branding but often looked and behaved differently.
User feedback was generally owned by Product and arrived late, often during UAT. Design choices frequently reflected stakeholder opinion instead of usability evidence. Product and Development also had low trust: Product saw Development as slow and difficult to work with, while Development felt Product did not account for technical realities. Teams built similar capabilities in silos, adding inconsistency and technical debt.

I was told I had been hired to “win arguments with the business.”

I believed the organization needed an intermediary who could understand business goals, make technical constraints visible, and advocate for end users. I saw Design as a way to help teams make better decisions together.

02Earning trust and establishing demand

I started by listening and building relationships across the company.
I offered design reviews, heuristic evaluations, office hours, and UX guidance to any team that wanted help. On the teams I supported directly, I introduced usability testing to ground decisions in observed user behavior.
That work built demand. Product owners began asking for more support than I could provide. I taught UX principles to interested colleagues and proposed hiring designers to the VP of Solution Development.
Usability testing surfaced a problem in an item lookup field. It was actually a search, but users didn’t know that. They entered a value and moved to the next field. Search latency meant results often appeared after users had completed the form and hit Submit. The form returned an error, and users didn’t realize they needed to return to the field and make a selection. Users made three or more unsuccessful attempts before learning the tool.
I added helper text, clarified the field label, and made the required selection clear. These fixes addressed the near-term need and showed that the lookup tool ultimately needed a redesign to account for system latency.
Across products, vague error messages also contributed to unnecessary support calls. The team introduced shared error language and standards.

03Scaling UX around portfolio priorities

The first UX hires were funded by product teams with the need and budget to add a designer. By late 2010, the capability had grown to six designers and a dedicated researcher. Until a Director of UX role was created in 2011, designers had functional rather than direct reporting relationships to me, but I owned hiring for UX roles, assigned UX work and resources across product teams, and set direction for UX work.
From 2011, I functioned as a principal designer: I was key in hiring, managed the UX internship program, and advised on resource allocation for junior designers. The practice evolved over several years, reaching eight designers at its peak in 2012 before a reduction in force brought the team to five in 2023.
Product-funded staffing tied each designer to one team’s budget, and that model stopped fitting as Premier acquired companies and priorities shifted: not every team continued to need a full-time designer, while other products had greater business need. I allocated UX support based on business priority and the capability each team needed. Teams that had reached a steady state could move from embedded support to a consultative model, freeing designers to work where they could have greater impact.
That shift required trust. Teams had come to think of designers as “their” resources, so I explained the shared portfolio goal, assured teams they could still get UX help, and sometimes provided interim support myself.

Example: Remitra. After Premier acquired the application, the product team resisted UX input and focused on superficial changes in response to market pressure. The product underperformed, and the business chose to invest elsewhere. With the business priority elsewhere and the team not acting on design input, a dedicated designer was no longer the best use of a limited capability. I moved the designer to another product and kept Remitra on consultative support: UX help as needed, plus direction to the design-system documentation.
Decisions like this one were the basis for my case that UX needed to be a team with the autonomy to allocate resources across the portfolio: designers managed as an enterprise capability, not as resources owned by the product team that first funded them. UX ultimately became its own team and moved into Product Management.

04Developing designers into strategic partners

I coached designers to understand the business priorities of their products and how those products fit into the wider portfolio.
My shorthand was that designers should be “chefs, not short-order cooks”: use judgment to turn business and user needs into thoughtful solutions rather than simply fulfill interface requests.
I supported that growth through one-on-one coaching, design reviews, team exercises, and an internship program.

Growing an early hire’s influence

He had strong visual design skills but lacked confidence and was often overruled in product meetings. The gap was not the quality of his work; it was his influence in the room.

I coached him to present design work with evidence and helped him build confidence. As his confidence grew, so did his ownership: he grew into leading envisioning workshops with senior leaders and partner health systems to reimagine the purchaser workflow across the supply chain suite. The workshops produced user journeys, a capability map, and a high-level prototype that we built together.

The result mattered beyond his own growth. The future-state concept influenced the product roadmap and helped make the case for supply chain modernization. These were the same envisioning workshops described in section 07, which helped secure C-suite funding to begin modernization with ERP.

During this period, I also coached a different designer whose work was often limited to screen edits and whose deliverables did not consistently align with design-system standards or implementation. 05Creating shared patterns that teams could adopt

The UX team audited Premier’s 40+ product portfolio and identified common interface patterns.
We partnered with UI Development to build Fireball, an internal toolkit with reusable code components. A team member with deep accessibility expertise developed the style guide, pattern library, and accessibility standards.
Adoption depended on the product and its technology. Informatics adopted Fireball across its products. Over roughly a year, Informatics reported reduced UI development time; standardized components also enabled QA automation that significantly reduced QA tasks. These improvements were reported, not quantified.
Supply Chain had more products on disparate technologies and codebases. Requiring teams to rewrite applications to adopt Fireball would have been lengthy and expensive. UX and UI Development created a code-free guide to shared behaviors, styles, and interactions. Supply Chain adopted the guidance in roughly 20% of its products.

06Returning to Premier: design system and modernization

I returned in 2017 as a Lead UX Designer. In 2023, after the reduction in force that brought the team to five designers, I took over the UX practice. The designers became my direct reports, and I took on full people- and resource-management responsibility: direct management, performance management, coaching and development, workload management, and allocation of UX resources.
The team was less engaged in vision work, so I focused on rebuilding that capability.
The portfolio’s continuing inconsistencies were becoming visible in sales conversations with health systems. UX had moved between Technology, Product, and Marketing over time. After UX moved under Marketing, its influence increased again, and Technology leadership decided Premier needed a shared design-system source of truth connected to code.
I helped make that case, led the UX side of the system’s development, and established its governance. A UX resource maintained Figma documentation, components, and patterns; UI Development built corresponding code components in Storybook. Any contributor could request a lightweight review of a new element or variation. The team could approve it as a standard addition, a variation, or an exception—or reject it. Approved changes went into the Design System backlog and were prioritized by impact and need. My team did much of the day-to-day system building.

07Turning a modernization goal into funded work

Supply Chain and ERP modernization became corporate goals. I proposed to Product Design leadership that UX support or lead an envisioning effort.
I worked with my team to plan the workshops, deliverables, and roadmap. I co-facilitated with a team member, the designer whose growth is described in section 04; the team created and presented journey maps and capability roadmaps with my support. I assigned team members to that work and to projects that emerged from it.
Product and Technology used the deliverables to shape a multi-generational roadmap—from a minimum viable product toward the desired future state. The work helped leaders understand what capabilities were needed at each stage and supported the funding case.
The purchaser-workflow vision helped establish ERP as the right place to begin supply chain modernization. Its antiquated interface and complex user experience were limiting sales. The C-suite approved funding to start the effort with ERP. That funding paid for revising core requisition workflows, applying the Premier Design System, and implementing its codebase. Senior leaders asked me to be hands-on with ERP.

08Leading ERP design while rebuilding the team

As Principal Product Designer for ERP, I worked to simplify a complex interface without losing its depth of capability.
Stakeholders were resistant to change, including adopting the design-system patterns. I used comparative prototypes to gather feedback and support design decisions; these were not formal A/B tests.
The ERP work covered four procure-to-pay workflows: dashboard, shop, requisitions, and approvals. It also included new pharmacy purchasing capabilities designed with Optum as the pilot client. The work was still in progress when I left Premier in March 2026, and outcome metrics had not yet been calculated.

10Leadership impact and reflection

Three kinds of leadership, aimed at the same goal: helping teams make better decisions together.

Strategic leadership

Connected business goals, technical realities, and user evidence; advocated for an autonomous UX practice; and helped shape and secure funding for modernization.

People leadership

Coached designers, created opportunities for them to lead, built an internship pipeline, and allocated talent as priorities changed.

Organizational leadership

Introduced research and shared practices, established design-system governance, and adapted resource and adoption models to different product needs.

Reflection

Trust was central to this work.
Teams had no mandate to adopt UX standards, so progress depended on listening, empathy, partnership, and evidence, whether I was introducing research, building shared patterns, or reallocating designers. Teams were more willing to change when they understood the shared goal, felt supported, and could see how the approach helped them do their work.

Premier hired me to win arguments with the business. Instead, I built a practice that helped the business make better decisions: designers who acted as strategic partners, standards and operating models that scaled across a portfolio, and design work that shaped where the company invested, through reorganizations and reductions in force.

That is the leadership I bring to any multi-product organization: balance customer needs with technical and commercial priorities, create consistency without adding process for its own sake, and stay close to the product.
